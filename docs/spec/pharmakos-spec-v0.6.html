Design specification · working title

Pharmakos

The cure and the poison are the same thing. Nothing arrives over the air.

Pharmakos is an open-source voxel strategy game about orders you cannot take back. Each seat seals a playbook during the planning Lull, then watches the Push play out like a movie: nobody has live control, and a commander must walk to a beacon and interface on site to change anything. In v1 everything that executes is built in — the commander's operator, the mandates on beacons, the programs in units and buildings — and everything you touch is data: playbooks and templates are JSONC files you edit in the in-game editor or by hand. Opponents are the built-in operator; external scripting and live AI seats are the roadmap, not v1.

What changed since draft 5

  • The game is Pharmakos, always paired with its subtitle: "Pharmakos: The Sealed Order". The scapegoat shares its root with pharmakon, remedy and poison in one object; AirGap survives as the codename and as the in-world name for the trust model.
  • v1 is simplified. Everything that executes is Rust at three levels: the commander's built-in operator, built-in mandates on beacons, built-in programs in units and buildings. Everything the player touches is data.
  • Playbooks and templates are JSONC files. No SDK, no script language, no script runtime, no published API, no MCP and no AI seats in v1; external scripting (v1.1) and the live conduit (v1.2) are designed for now through reserved proto seams.
  • One checkpoint, not two: the wk-17.5 fun probe is deleted and the single gate at wk 35.5 is also the v0.1 public release. v1 is the hardened build at wk 63.5, about 18 months with buffer.
  • Power: the beacon is the unit of power. A dormant beacon powers down everything homed to it; seal, sphere, interfacing and recycling stay powered by the key-core, and the core autocannon runs off the deep bore. One Generator per vent, output set by richness.
  • Sphere is the only range for physical work; reach is only what a seat knows. The enemy-overlap ban is deleted: build, dig and deploy are legal anywhere your own sphere covers, enemy spheres included, and combat decides.
  • Money: value follows condition (build cost × current HP) everywhere value is read; paid means yours the moment an order commits; the Ledger — the armistice engine that pays and fines every seat (section 2) — settles at each recap; one catch-up dial (Basic Minimum Income scaled by rank on held value) and no award-fund rank multiplier; kill credit is clamped to HP removed and dropped on elimination.
  • Match end is a tick rule: conditions are checked every tick, the Push halts at the first tick with fewer than two seats, and there is no draw state. An eliminated human keeps the full map or picks "Abandon match (no winner)".
  • Seams are finite, and a fabricator produces only while its mandate has outstanding work its drones cannot cover — headroom is the normal state.
  • Commander package: speed is a world constant; repair drones heal it; the death counter is per-Push and a respawn always completes by segment end; it is not an asset and despawns on elimination.
  • Plan execution contract: one rule body at a time, and only the reflex (commander HP ≤ 20%, fixed) interrupts; interface rows are atomic. The v1 vocabulary is exactly what the editor renders — flags, branch and repeat leave v1 with their field numbers reserved.
  • Player-written Dispatches — seat-to-seat notes — are cut from v1; a one-way chatter ticker of engine-authored lines takes their place. They return in v1.2.
  • The watch rig moves into the skeleton (own-fog camera, event list, 2–4× speed, skip, full map on elimination or match end), and segments run a fixed ladder by round number, 3 min up to 8, which the host can replace with a per-round length list: a single value sets every round, several values are taken round by round, and the last entry repeats.
  • Fog is a per-match server-side policy applied by the gateway's fog filter; live standings show your own score and rank only.
  • Stages are re-cut around the gate, the nightly league becomes three nightly adversarial scenarios (Rusher, Turtle, Hunter), and the gate criteria are written down in section 16.
  • Art, audio and packaging are decided: MagicaVoxel or Goxel to .vox through the same greedy mesher, CC0/CC-BY audio, ~1 wk of packaging in the skeleton and ~3 wk of signed installers in hardening.

1Vision & scope

Pharmakos is a playable game first, and an AI platform by direction: it is built so that AI control fits the design from the start rather than being bolted on later, but v1 ships nothing that executes except the game itself. It has to be fun to play and worth watching. v1 wears a placeholder voxel look; the art pass is roadmap. One developer builds it with AI coding agents, so scope discipline is a design constraint in its own right.

Draft 6 The theme. A pharmakon is remedy and poison in one object, and security tooling has the same duality: the same tool patches and breaks. So the question the game asks is who or what you can trust, and its answer is that trust is engineered, not assumed — orders are sealed, the seal is inspected, nobody has live control, and the game never holds your keys. The air gap is the physical form of that answer; section 2 tells it as the world's story and section 4 as the commander's legs.

2World & lore

Decided Pharmakos is set in the Handfast world: grim and near-future, lightened by bureaucratic humour in the Ledger's voice. This is original IP and borrows no franchise names.

Draft 6 The title is PHARMAKOS, always shipped as a lockup — the fixed title-and-subtitle pairing "PHARMAKOS: THE SEALED ORDER" (the subtitle is a working one), never the bare word. AirGap survives as the codename and as the in-world term for the trust model. A 2026-09-13 sweep found no PHARMAKOS mark in classes 9, 28 and 41 on TMview, an empty UK register and no Steam result. Live risks: PHARMAKOS – The Lunghouse (Howling Stars, an itch jam build of January 2026, studio Steam-verified, no store page yet), and drift towards "Pharmakon", which is a live Steam puzzle game and a German class 9 mark. Actions: register the pharmakosgame handles and domains now, and a professional clearance search during hardening, before v1 (not legal advice). Exposure: v0.1 ships on these sweeps alone, so a search that fails after v0.1 means renaming a released build along with its domains, handles and early press. Faction names stay provisional; all world terms live in the table below, so a rename is a find-and-replace.

Draft 6 The theme, in the world's own terms. A pharmakos is the scapegoat a city feeds and honours, then drives out so the city stays clean — a role, not a person; it shares its root with pharmakon, the remedy that is also the poison (section 1). Here that duality takes a physical form, the air gap. The commander is the physical courier of that trust chain: an order reaches a beacon only because a body walks there and interfaces on site — touch to change. Nothing over the air reconfigures anything. Radio exists only once a Mast is earned, and even then it carries data messages that a Steward (a seat's operator) may read and must never obey.

By 2061 humans and machine minds share every grid, farm and factory. Then the Overwrite shows that any machine that listens can be rewritten by a cleverly shaped signal. Civilisation splits along a line of belief, not species. The war that follows ends in the Pall, a sky of ash and dust that has not lifted in a generation. Solar is a memory, so every cell — every settlement — lives on geothermal heat: the deep bore under a founding Stake, the shallow bore that keeps each key-core's seal lit, and the vents worth fighting over. The Quorum is a sovereign mind governed by the votes of millions of human citizens. The Handfast Line is made of human cells and the minds sworn to serve them, and it refuses to be ruled by anything it can't unplug. Both sides field humans and AIs. Nobody gives orders over radio any more: a Keybearer carries an air-gapped key and presses it to a Stake's key-core by hand. The only thing both sides still obey is the Ledger, a pre-war armistice engine that pays every cell, fines the incompetent in deadpan memos, and inspects every set of sealed orders.

The frame explains the core mechanics:

  • Mirror matches. Each seat picks a banner, which is cosmetic, and feuding cells explain any mix of seats.
  • No live control. Radio silence during the Push is the reason playbooks are sealed, and the reason the commander has to travel.
  • Draft 6 Untrusted radio. Every message on the air carries its sender and its Mast of origin, and is read as data. Stewards may listen, but no pathway lets them obey. Interception, spoofing, jamming and their counters are roadmap (section 18).
  • Awards and safe playbooks. The Ledger gives these out; a safe playbook is "a standing order filed on your behalf". Citations come after v1.
  • Decided Darkness and power. The Pall explains why there is no solar, why vents are worth fighting over, and why lit domes and brownouts are so visible on screen.
  • Decided Licences. Capabilities are Ledger licences, earned by meeting its conditions; the conditions are checked at the settlement and announced in the recap.
  • Decided Bores. Only a seat's founding core sits on a deep bore with spare heat. Other key-cores tap just enough to keep their own seal lit. When a core falls, its bore collapses for good.
  • Draft 6 Chatter. A site is never quiet. Stewards call to their crews in short lines, and you overhear that talk as the seal closes on a Push and again at the recap. It is one-way flavour, written by the engine, and it never carries an order. Player-written Dispatches come back after v1.
  • Draft 6 Ruins, settlement and the final audit. When a Stake falls — one beacon or a whole seat — everything homed to it becomes neutral ruins: inert, unrepairable, worth nothing to the audit, salvageable by anyone. The Ledger settles at each recap, paying the ration and the award fund. At the round limit the final audit credits what you hold, in the state it stands in, and what you broke.

  • Draft 6 Round-limit winner, "the final audit": score = value held after the final settlement ($ plus the build cost of your beacons, structures and units, each counted at build cost × current HP) + the build cost of enemy assets you destroyed. An asset lost with no enemy damage on the clock — self-inflicted, terrain, disbanded on elimination, ruins — credits nobody, and an eliminated seat's accrued share is dropped rather than redistributed. Shares are apportioned as integers by largest remainder, ties to the lowest seat id. The recap shows "shared kill". Decided Tie-break order: unsmoothed net worth at the final audit, then enemy value destroyed, then fewer beacons lost, then a shared win. Risk kingmaking, farming the weakest seat and turtling on a hoard in 3-way free-for-all; watched by the three nightly adversarial scenarios and at the gate.

  • Draft 6 During play: no manual control of anything. The human gets one camera rig — an own-fog camera with free-look and a follow-commander toggle — plus a live event list, 2–4× speed, and skip, which jumps to segment end and is free and instant. 4Commander

Draft 6 Touch to change. The commander is the physical courier of the trust chain. An order reaches a beacon only when a body walks there and interfaces on site; nothing over the air can reconfigure anything. A Radio Mast, once earned, carries data messages and go-codes — never orders — and that data is treated as untrusted. The air gap is not a setting: it is the commander's legs.
  • Decided Role: the commander is the only unit a seat drives directly, and only through its playbook. It is weak and killable. Draft 6 It has no passive regeneration; repair drones repair it like any other friendly unit.
  • Decided Movement: a hover-walker that uses the same locomotion as everything else in v1 (section 9): it walks, climbs 1-voxel steps, and is blocked by walls, craters and trenches, so terrain, ramps and bridges shape its routes. Draft 6 Its speed is a world constant, not a per-seat stat; the starting range is about 1–1.5 voxels per second (Tuning). Map scale is quoted in commander travel as well as raider travel: spawns sit at least about 1.5 early Pushes of raider travel apart, which is several minutes of commander walking.
  • Decided Death: respawns after a delay (for example 30 s, growing with each death) at the core; if the core is gone, at the surviving beacon nearest to where it died. Beacons keep running, but nothing can change them until the commander returns. Death also interrupts any visit in progress. Playbooks must include an on_death block. Draft 6 The death counter and its growing delay are per-Push state, reset each segment, and a pending respawn always completes by segment end — so every Lull snapshot has a live commander at a known place. Tuning delay curve.
  • Draft 6 Not an asset: the commander has no build cost and never appears in held value or kill credit. It despawns at the tick its seat is eliminated. Killing one pays only in the victim's lost interface time — which is the whole point of hunting it.
  • Decided Hunting: commanders are fair targets and hunting them is a real strategy. Attack can select the enemy commander from its last sighting (freshness is tested at plan time and the target is pinned at seal, section 6) and retargets live while any of its units can see it. Defences: routes that avoid known threats, flee rules, on_death, the fixed reflex at 20% HP (section 10), and a Defend beacon guarding "my commander". Further counters (armour, decoys, a self-defence weapon) are added only if the nightly Hunter scenario shows hunting dominates. Risk
  • Decided Start: each seat begins with a pre-placed core beacon (Build mandate) in its spawn zone, the commander, 2 build drones, 1 mining drone and $ equal to 2 rounds of BMI. The core's key-core powers that force with some headroom, and a heat vent sits a short walk away. The core is armistice-hardened: high HP and a built-in autocannon fed by its deep bore, so a single early raid can't eliminate a seat. Tuning starting numbers.
  • Roadmap Self-defence weapon, carrying a demolition charge, minimal dig/construct, sensor ping, vehicles with auto-firing defences (earned), and manual control for a full round.

10Playbooks

Draft 6 A playbook is typed declarative data, not a script: a route of steps, prioritised rules, a required on_death block and a required fallback. It drives the commander for one segment. The human writes one in the editor and the built-in operator generates one; both produce the same file.

Format and vocabulary

  • Draft 6 File: a playbook is a JSONC file — canonical proto JSON for the gp.v1 package, plus comments. It is hand-editable in any text editor, and the editor round-trips comments and formatting, so a hand-written file survives being opened and saved. Templates and the sample library are the same format. There is no script language, no SDK and no script runtime in v1; external scripts are Roadmap (section 18).
  • Naming: the schema package is gp.v1 and the gateway methods keep the word plan (verify_plan, submit_plan, render_plan). Everywhere else the thing is a playbook.
  • Draft 6 The v1 vocabulary is exactly what the editor renders: route steps with guards, handlers (rules), selectors, on_death, fallback and options. set_flag, clear_flag, branch, repeat and the flag predicates are not in v1; their field numbers are reserved and they return in v1.1 with the expert editor view (section 18), as a minor version with no converter.
  • Draft 6 Load never strips: an out-of-vocabulary construct is rejected with a verifier error carrying a code and a JSON Pointer, so an edited file either loads as written or says why it did not.

Execution

  • Timing: playbooks run inside the deterministic sim at 20 Hz. Decisions (rules, step transitions, selector resolution) are taken every 250 ms of game time. Times in the file are game milliseconds, never ticks.
  • Draft 6 One body at a time: on each decision the first enabled rule whose condition is true starts its body. At most one rule body runs at a time and no rule pre-empts another; while none is running, the current route step continues.
  • Draft 6 Only the reflex interrupts. It fires at commander HP ≤ 20% — fixed, not disableable, and there is no reflex_hp_pct option. It aborts any visit in progress, walks the commander to the safest own beacon, and then the route continues from the step it was on. It re-fires only after new damage. Author-tuned bravery is an ordinary flee rule, not a setting.
  • Draft 6 Interface rows are atomic: each row is one change that commits at the end of its own duration, so a multi-field edit is all-or-nothing. Committed rows stay; a resumed visit is a new visit and pays the handshake again.
  • Draft 6 Segment end: the playbook stops, and nothing carries over except the world itself. The coming segment's length in milliseconds is part of the frozen snapshot, so the editor clock, the fits pill and render_plan all read the same number rather than a constant.

Why a playbook survives a long segment without a dry run

  1. Late-bound selectors. For example "my weakest Defend beacon", or "the nearest known enemy beacon within the reach window". They resolve when the step starts, then stay pinned for that step.
  2. Rules with explicit resume semantics: a body runs to its end, then continue, skip step, jump forward, or end route.
  3. Guards on every step, so steps degrade instead of blocking.
  4. Autonomy on the beacon side, set on site. For example, an Attack launches at 4:00 or on go-code A, so a dead commander doesn't stall it.
  5. A guaranteed tail: the fallback posture (hold, shadow or patrol), and the self-preservation reflex.

Conditions

  • Structure: all / any / not trees, depth ≤4 and ≤24 nodes, integer comparisons only.
  • Draft 6 Knowledge: conditions read only the seat's knowledge store. Enemy predicates must declare freshness (VISIBLE_NOW or KNOWN_WITHIN ms); Attack target selectors take their freshness from the seat's reach memory N (Tuning) instead, so there is one reach window and one verifier check.
  • Predicate families: clock; commander (HP, position, deaths, recent damage); own beacon (status, HP, under attack, units by role, mandate, powered or dormant, structures, reports since); aggregates; enemy knowledge (units near, beacon known, structure near); economy (treasury, income forecast, kW supply, draw and headroom); capability licence and structure state; mast coverage; playbook state (step reached, rule fired, posture).
  • Deliberately absent: enemy playbooks, enemy treasury and mandates, unseen positions, enemy intent, and randomness.

Limits and termination

Every playbook halts. These limits are technical (bounded cost and guaranteed termination), and in the story they are the Ledger's seal-inspection rules:

  • Jumps and waits: a jump only ever goes forward, and every wait has a timeout.
  • Decided Size: one playbook size budget replaces the separate counts. Steps, rules and their bodies, build targets and protected areas all count toward it. It is sized so FULL verification stays under 50 ms and every seat's rules fit the 20 Hz tick. The embedded gate P1 and the per-rule cost on a decision tick set the number. The editor and verifier show a size meter. There are no beginner or expert rule tiers.
  • Rule firing: each rule has a cooldown ≥5 s and fires at most 1–8 times.
  • Hard stop: the end of the segment.

The per-seat evaluation cost is a constant.

13Playbook editor

Decided Humans author playbooks with three tools, built on the same Seat Gateway every other client uses: a map timeline, a gambit-style rule list, and templates. It draws on Frozen Synapse waypoints, Door Kickers go-codes, FF12 gambits, Gladiabots, Into the Breach telegraphing and Factorio share strings.

  • Map route:
    • Click one of your beacons to choose Visit & change, Go here or Recycle. Click the ground to Go here or Place beacon, with a live legality ghost. Click a known enemy beacon to target it with an Attack beacon.
    • Alt-click turns a fixed target into a selector.
    • Routes draw as polylines with travel estimates; legs through fog are dashed.
  • Segment clock: bars for move, interface (with a tick at each change's commit point), placement, wait, fallback and slack. Worst-case rule detours show in a second lane. A "fits" pill turns green, amber or red. Scrubbing moves a ghost commander along the schedule preview only; outcomes are never shown. Draft 6 The clock and the pill are scaled to the coming segment's length, taken from the frozen snapshot rather than assumed (the ladder in section 3).
  • Rule list: each rule is a sentence with clickable parameter chips, reordered by dragging. Conditions are picked through family → predicate → parameters. Vocabulary you haven't unlocked shows greyed out with a lock and the reason. Conflicting rules are joined by a connector line.
  • Templates (8): Hold & Build, Expand & Mine, Fortify Under Threat, Economy Reset, Staged Attack with Go-Time (Decided optional Survey post), Capability Rush, Beacon Chain Push, Safe Playbook. Each is a parameter wizard pre-filled with the built-in operator's suggestion and a "why" note.
  • Draft 6 Local-first library: templates and a sample library are JSONC files in a local folder. You can copy one and edit it by hand, or use Save as template / Load in the editor. Nothing in the library executes: these are data files, not scripts. The gateway reads that folder to list and instantiate templates, but stores none of it: your files stay local, and anything you write reaches the gateway only once it becomes a draft or is submitted. The shipped samples carry description headers so a reader can tell at a glance what each one is for.
  • Draft 6 The editor's reach is the schema: the v1 playbook vocabulary is exactly what this editor renders — route steps with guards, handlers, selectors, on_death, fallback and options. Load rejects any out-of-vocabulary construct with a verifier code and a JSON Pointer to the offending node; it never silently strips what it cannot draw. Flags, branch, repeat and the raw-JSON view return together in v1.1 (section 18).
  • Draft 6 Draft continuity: each Lull opens with last round's playbook pre-loaded as an editable draft, re-verified against the new snapshot so anything the round invalidated shows as a diagnostic straight away.
  • Decided Notes box: the human's view of the seat notebook, read and written through the gateway like every other editor feature.
  • Validation: QUICK runs on every edit and FULL after 600 ms idle. Rows show icons and plain sentences; Fix buttons apply the machine-applicable patches the verifier offers. Draft 6 Typing feel is judged by P6, not by the verifier gate: a template playbook filed in ≤ 2 min and one from scratch in ≤ 5 min (section 16).
  • Exact round-trip: the editor's model is the playbook JSON. Edits are JSON Patches with inverse-patch undo, and comments survive a load-and-save unchanged. The plan core's canonical form is authoritative, and golden tests byte-compare the output.
  • Decided One editor view in v1: no rule-slot tiers. The beginner template starts with 4 rules, and a size meter shows the playbook budget.   • Accessibility: accessible names generated from the rendered sentences, icons plus text for severity, 100–200% font scaling, reduced motion.

14Built-in operator

Draft 6 Every commander is run by the built-in operator. It does three jobs: it generates a playbook with the rudimentary tool (templates plus utility scoring), it files the safe playbook when a seat submits nothing verified before the Lull ends, and it executes whatever playbook the seat sealed — the human's edited playbook and its own alike. A computer opponent is that operator with no human editing it, plus the built-in mandates on its beacons and the built-in programs in its units and buildings.

The operator is an ordinary client of the Seat Gateway (section 12): same snapshot, same verifier, same submit path, no privileged reads. It is deterministic — its seed comes from the match, seat and round, and its budget is measured in evaluation units rather than wall time.

  1. Read the briefing, beacons, known enemies, economy and capabilities.
  2. Compute integer situation features: threat, power headroom, vent sites, expansion sites, capability and attack opportunities, and commander risk.
  3. Fill template parameters with the top-k options, then compose visit goals by greedy insertion by utility per second until the route fills its target share of the segment (the segment's length comes from the snapshot).
  4. Score utility as defence + economy + expansion + capability + attack − risk, weighted by difficulty. Decided v1 ships the Balanced weighting only; the Turtle, Raider and Economist personalities are Roadmap.
  5. Add the standard rules, then run verify and repair up to 4 times.

Draft 6 Target spread: the operator scores a target lower by how much pressure any other seat is already putting on it, the human included, so two built-in seats do not pile onto the same seat by default. There is no standings term: catch-up lives in BMI scaling alone (section 7).

Draft 6 Chatter: on a built-in seat the operator emits short engine-authored lines from its intent and recent events (for example "Expanding north. Stay clear."), sometimes misleading. It never reads chatter — chatter is one-way, and nothing over the air changes a beacon. A seat a human is editing emits none: the human's intent never reaches the ticker. The lobby labels the seat "built-in".

Draft 6 Safe playbook: leave configurations untouched and move to the safest beacon. If power is short and up to 2 at-risk beacons are within 60 s travel, raise their Quartermaster priority (nearest first; never recycle, switch mandate, or place) so they brown out last. Otherwise shadow the safest beacon, with the flee and rescue rules. It always qualifies, and the editor renders it so a seat can see the cost of a timeout.

Draft 6 Mandates and programs: below the operator, every beacon mandate runs its built-in program (section 6) and every unit and building runs its built-in program. All three levels are Rust in v1 and the player edits none of them; the playbook is the only thing a seat authors. The proto reserves the seam at each level — operator {builtin|script}, mandate {builtin|script}, program {builtin|script} — with only the builtin arm implemented. Roadmap The script arm, local models and AI seats belong to v1.1 and v1.2 (section 18).
